Your most credible creators may already work for you.

Founders and employees can carry expertise a brand page cannot manufacture. The opportunity is real, but only when participation is chosen, the story is useful and the commercial relationship is clear.

By Henk PretoriusFounder / Campaign Producer

Most businesses are already sitting on the raw material for their strongest content. It lives in the founder who remembers why the company was started, the engineer who can explain the hard problem, the service lead who hears the same customer fear every week and the employee who knows what actually happens after the sale.

Then marketing gets involved and removes the person. The experience is compressed into a polished company post, the language becomes safe and the point of view disappears. The result may be accurate, but it sounds like everybody else. If people connect with people, the answer is not to manufacture more personality for the brand. It is to help the right people speak with clarity and purpose.

Turn willing experts into useful voices, not content inventory. Give them strategic support, protect their choice and make the commercial relationship clear.

The opportunity is expertise with a name

The 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer describes a world retreating into smaller circles of trust. Its global study covered 33,938 respondents across 28 countries. One of its findings is that a trusted social voice can make people reconsider a company they currently distrust. That does not prove every employee should become an influencer. It does show that the relationship carrying a message can change how the message is received.

In B2B, the people consuming useful thinking are not always the obvious buyer. Edelman and LinkedIn's 2025 study drew on nearly 2,000 professionals and focused on hidden buyers inside purchasing groups. LinkedIn reports that 55 per cent of hidden buyers use thought leadership during vendor evaluation, almost identical to the 56 per cent recorded among target buyers. A credible expert can reach people the sales team may never meet.

Do not turn people into posting targets

The quickest way to destroy this opportunity is to treat employees as a cheaper distribution network. A target of three posts a week may create output, but it does not create a reason to listen. People become anxious about saying the wrong thing, managers start approving every sentence and the feed fills with minor variations of the company message.

Start with willing participants who have something useful to say and a clear reason to say it. Some will be comfortable on camera. Others will be stronger in conversation, writing, audio or short demonstrations. The format should follow the person and the audience. Participation should never become an unspoken performance requirement.

  • Choose expertise and willingness before reach or job title.
  • Set a useful audience job for each voice, not a weekly posting quota.
  • Give people professional support without making every person sound the same.

Build stories from the work already happening

The material is usually close at hand: a mistake that changed the process, a customer question that keeps returning, a decision the team had to defend, a belief shaped by experience or a useful way to understand an industry problem. These are not private diary entries. They are specific human routes into problems the right customer already cares about.

Do not ask every expert to become a copywriter. Interview them. Find the moment, tension or observation only they can supply. Then shape it into a clear structure: what happened, why it matters to the audience, what the person learned and what the reader can do with that lesson. The final piece should preserve the speaker's judgement and language while earning the audience's time.

  • Start with one real event, question or decision.
  • Connect it to a problem the intended customer recognises.
  • Offer a useful lesson before asking for attention or action.

Sources: 2, 3

One voice still needs one campaign direction

Personal content does not mean disconnected content. The campaign owner should define the commercial objective, intended audience, message territory, evidence and boundaries. The participant brings the lived experience and point of view. A writer, producer or editor helps turn that material into the right format without removing what made it worth hearing.

This is a production system, not a collection of spontaneous posts. One strong conversation can become a considered article, a short video, a sales follow-up, a podcast segment and several focused social pieces. Each asset needs its own job, but the story should move the same relationship forward. Repetition becomes useful when the idea stays consistent and the expression changes with the context.

Sources: 2, 3

Participation must be specific and reversible

An employee's face, voice and story are not just production assets. They involve personal information, employment relationships and usage rights. The UK Information Commissioner's Office warns that consent in employment can be difficult because of the power imbalance between employer and worker. If consent is the chosen lawful basis, it must be freely given, specific, informed and as easy to withdraw as it was to give.

The ICO gives a directly relevant example: when a worker withdraws consent for their image to appear in marketing materials, the organisation must remove it as soon as possible and should erase it if requested. Build that reality into the system before filming. Agree channels, territories, duration, paid use, editing, reuse after employment and any AI treatment of image or voice. Record approvals and create a workable withdrawal process. This is operational guidance, not a substitute for legal advice on the organisation's chosen lawful basis.

  • Make participation optional and refusal consequence-free.
  • Approve the final content and the intended uses before release.
  • Define what happens when a person changes role, leaves or withdraws consent.

Sources: 4

Trust requires visible commercial transparency

A personal account does not remove the commercial relationship. Current ASA guidance says that when someone is personally or commercially connected to a brand, including as an employee or director, content featuring or referring to the brand must be obviously identifiable as advertising. The audience should not have to inspect a biography or remember an earlier post to understand the connection.

Treat disclosure as part of the creative, not small print added at the end. Decide which posts are promotional, label them clearly and place the label where the audience will see it before engaging. Keep claims within what the person and company can support. Transparency does not weaken a credible voice. Hidden interest does.

  • Identify promotional employee posts before they are published.
  • Use a clear, prominent advertising label where the guidance requires it.
  • Keep evidence for product, service and performance claims.

Sources: 5

What this means for your next campaign

Begin with one willing expert and one important customer problem. Record a proper conversation, find the story that reveals useful experience and build a small set of assets around one campaign objective. Agree the person's choices, rights and approvals before production. Make any commercial relationship obvious. Then measure whether the work starts useful conversations, improves understanding, supports sales and brings the right customers back. Your people do not need to imitate full-time influencers. They need help turning what they know into stories the right audience can trust and use.
