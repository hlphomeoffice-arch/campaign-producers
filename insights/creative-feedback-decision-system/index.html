Creative feedback is a decision system, not a comments thread.

Better creative review is not about collecting more reactions. It is about deciding what the work must do, who decides and which evidence matters.

By Henk PretoriusFounder / Campaign Producer

Creative review often looks collaborative while behaving like a queue of private opinions. A link circulates, comments accumulate and the team tries to reconcile reactions that answer different questions. One person is judging strategy, another is correcting copy, another is managing internal risk and another is choosing what they personally like. More feedback arrives, but the decision becomes less clear.

The BetterIdeas Project studied 1,034 marketers, agency people and in-house practitioners across 54 countries. It found that an idea typically takes five rounds of development to reach approval. Only a small minority said ideas were always assessed against clear criteria, while most respondents believed personal opinion had a large influence on decisions. The answer is not to silence useful voices. It is to design a review process that knows what each voice is for.

Set the decision, criteria and authority before the review. Then turn many useful inputs into one clear direction for the creative team.

Name the decision before opening the work

Every review should begin with the decision required at that stage. Are we choosing a strategic route, selecting a campaign idea, improving an approved execution or checking production readiness? When those decisions are mixed, feedback travels backwards. A legal copy question can reopen the idea. A personal reaction to colour can distract from whether the route answers the brief.

State what is in scope and what is already settled. Give reviewers the brief, the relevant criteria and the options available: approve, approve with specified changes, request a defined revision or reject with a reason linked to the brief. This turns the meeting from a performance of reactions into a useful decision point.

  • Decision now: write the exact choice the group must make.
  • Already agreed: list the points that are not being reopened.
  • Criteria: show how the work will be judged at this stage.
  • Next action: name what each possible decision will trigger.

Judge the idea against agreed criteria

Personal response is useful evidence, but it is not a complete evaluation method. A reviewer may dislike an idea that is strategically sharp, or enjoy work that cannot move the intended audience. Translate reactions into questions the team can examine. What in the brief does this concern relate to? Is it a risk, a preference or new information? What change would improve the campaign job?

The BetterIdeas research found that only 10 per cent of respondents said ideas were always evaluated against clearly defined criteria. Create those criteria before ideas arrive. They might include audience relevance, clarity of the intended change, brand ownership, channel potential, feasibility and the ability to be measured. Keep the list short enough to guide a real choice.

Separate input from approval authority

People can add value to a review without all holding equal approval power. The sales lead may bring customer knowledge. Legal may identify an unacceptable claim. Media may explain how an idea behaves in context. Finance may clarify a limit. The accountable campaign owner must weigh those inputs and make one decision that preserves the commercial objective.

Map these roles before the first review: who recommends, who advises, who checks a specialist risk and who gives final approval. WFA research on creative excellence points to too many decision makers as a continuing organisational barrier. Clear authority is not about making one person infallible. It is about preventing an unresolved committee from becoming the creative director.

  • Contributors supply evidence or specialist judgement.
  • The campaign lead consolidates implications and protects the brief.
  • The approver makes the stated decision within an agreed deadline.
  • A late entrant can reopen work only with material new information.

Sources: 2

Give the team one coherent direction

Do not ask the creative team to interpret a wall of conflicting comments. The accountable lead should consolidate feedback, resolve contradictions and explain priority. Required changes must be separated from suggestions. Each required change should include the problem it solves, while leaving the specialist room to propose the best creative answer.

Good consolidation is not a summary of every sentence. It is a decision about what matters. If one reviewer asks for greater surprise and another asks for more explanation, the lead must return to the campaign job and decide which need has priority, or how both can be answered without weakening the idea. Sending both comments onwards simply transfers leadership work to the wrong people.

Sources: 1, 2

Treat extra rounds as a diagnostic signal

Another round may be necessary, but it should not be automatic. Before requesting it, identify what remains unresolved. Is the idea weak, the brief unclear, the evidence incomplete, the approver absent or the organisation uncomfortable with a decision it has already made? Different causes require different fixes. Reworking the execution cannot repair an argument about strategy.

The BetterIdeas study reported an average of five development rounds and described approval as slow, inconsistent and subjective. Track why rounds occur, not only how many. If the same issue returns, pause the work and settle it with the people who own the underlying decision. This protects time, morale and budget without lowering the standard.

  • Record the unresolved question before commissioning another round.
  • Bring the decision owner into the room if authority is missing.
  • Rebrief when the strategic problem has changed.
  • Revise the work only when the creative answer is the real issue.

Sources: 1

Protect the campaign thread across every format

Feedback continues after the central idea is approved. Adaptations for social, film, display, events, sales materials and other touchpoints each introduce practical demands. Review them against two things at once: the specific job of that format and the campaign idea that must remain recognisable. Consistency is not copying the same execution into every space.

Kantar reports that connected campaign effects have become a larger contributor to performance, and argues that the creative idea should provide the thread across channels. This makes coordinated review essential. Specialists need freedom to use their format well, while one accountable lead protects the common audience change, message and brand memory. Fragmented feedback can optimise individual assets while quietly dismantling the campaign.

Sources: 3

What this means for your next campaign

For the next creative review, send a one-page decision note with the work. Name the decision, the criteria, what is already agreed, who contributes and who approves. During the review, turn reactions into evidence, risks or preferences. Afterwards, issue one prioritised direction with a reason for every required change. The aim is not fewer opinions at any cost. It is a campaign that benefits from specialist input without losing clarity, pace or accountability. Creative feedback becomes useful when the team knows exactly how it will become a decision.
