Brief the commercial change, not the list of deliverables.

A list of assets tells people what to make. A useful campaign brief tells them what must change, why it matters and how the work will be judged.

By Henk PretoriusFounder / Campaign Producer

Many campaign briefs arrive looking complete. They contain a launch date, a channel list, a budget range and a tidy table of deliverables. Yet the central decision is still missing: what must this campaign change for the business and for the people it needs to reach? Without that answer, a team can produce every requested asset and still fail to solve the problem.

This is not a small administrative gap. The global BetterBriefs study gathered views from more than 1,700 marketers and agency people across over 70 countries. Respondents estimated that poor briefs and misdirected work waste about a third of marketing budgets. The same study found a sharp perception gap between the people writing briefs and the people receiving them. Better briefing is therefore a commercial discipline, not a nicer template.

Define the commercial movement and audience change first. The right idea, specialists, formats and budget can then follow from the job.

Start with the movement the business needs

A commercial objective such as growth, awareness or engagement is too broad to direct a campaign. It names a desirable result without identifying the movement that will create it. A stronger brief makes the change specific: which audience must do, believe or understand something differently, and what business result should follow if that change happens?

That movement creates a line through the whole campaign. It helps strategy decide what matters, gives creative people a problem worth solving and gives production a reason for every format. It also exposes requests that do not belong. If an asset cannot help create the stated change, it should have to earn its place rather than entering the plan by habit.

  • What must be different in the business after this campaign has worked?
  • Whose behaviour, belief or understanding has to move first?
  • What evidence will tell us that the movement is happening?
  • What is outside the campaign's power and should not be promised?

Describe the audience change, not an audience label

Demographics and job titles can help locate people, but they rarely explain how to move them. A useful audience definition includes the current state, the barrier and the desired next step. A founder who knows a product category but doubts the cost of switching needs different work from one who has never heard of the category, even if both sit in the same segment.

The brief should therefore state what the audience currently thinks or does, why that state persists and what credible shift the campaign can ask for. UK briefing research reported that target group clarity was weak for both marketers and agencies. Replacing a broad label with a precise audience tension gives every specialist a shared human problem, not simply a media target.

Sources: 2

Separate facts, assumptions and decisions

Briefs become confusing when evidence, internal beliefs and instructions are written in the same tone. The team cannot tell what is known, what needs testing and what has already been decided. Label the three. Facts should be traceable to research or performance data. Assumptions should be open to challenge. Decisions should name the person who has authority to change them.

This simple separation makes the briefing more honest and more productive. Specialists can concentrate their challenge where uncertainty remains instead of reopening every settled point. It also prevents an opinion from acquiring the status of evidence through repetition. When new information appears, the team can see whether it changes an assumption, a decision or the original commercial movement.

  • Facts: verified information the team can rely on.
  • Assumptions: beliefs that may be useful but still need testing.
  • Decisions: chosen boundaries, with a named owner and reason.
  • Questions: important gaps that must be resolved before production.

Use constraints to shape the team and the work

A good constraint sharpens judgement. Budget, timing, legal requirements, brand commitments, available evidence and production access all affect what deserves to be made. Hiding them until an idea is approved does not create freedom. It creates rework. Put material constraints in the brief early, then invite the team to find the strongest answer inside them.

Once the campaign job and constraints are clear, team design becomes more precise. You can select the strategist, creative lead, writer, designer, director, editor, media specialist or technical partner because the result requires their contribution. You are no longer assembling a default roster and finding tasks for everyone. This protects budget while giving each specialist a clear responsibility.

Sources: 1, 2

Treat briefing as a working session

The document matters, but the briefing is where shared understanding is tested. Bring the people who own the commercial objective, the campaign decision and the specialist response into the same conversation. Ask them to restate the problem in their own words. Differences that sound minor on paper often reveal conflicting expectations about audience, ambition, risk or approval.

End the session by recording what changed, what remains open and who resolves each open point. Then issue one clean brief as the source of truth. BetterBriefs distinguishes the written brief from the act of briefing for good reason. Sending a file distributes information. A disciplined conversation creates alignment, exposes weak logic and gives the team a common standard for judging the work.

  • Confirm one commercial movement and one primary audience change.
  • Challenge assumptions before they harden into production instructions.
  • Agree evaluation criteria and approval authority in the room.
  • Publish the final decisions in one controlled version of the brief.

Sources: 2, 3

What this means for your next campaign

Before commissioning an asset, test the brief in one sentence: We need this audience to make this change so the business can achieve this result. If the sentence is vague, another deliverables table will not rescue it. Resolve the commercial movement, audience barrier, evidence, constraints and decision rights first. Then assemble only the specialists the campaign needs and let each format follow from a clear job. The shortest useful brief is not the one with the fewest words. It is the one that removes the most ambiguity.
